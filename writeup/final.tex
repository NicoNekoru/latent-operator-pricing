\begin{abstract}
    Financial markets exhibit complex, non-stationary dynamics (``regimes'') that traditional parametric models like Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) struggle to capture. We propose a Neural Operator framework that learns a low-dimensional latent manifold of market dynamics by solving the inverse problem of mapping historical market states to option price surfaces. Using a semi-synthetic ``Heston World'' ground truth, we demonstrate that the learned latent space topologically organizes into distinct volatility regimes without explicit supervision. Our model achieves a regime separation ratio of 4.31x, comparable to a volatility-explicit baseline (4.75x). Furthermore, we introduce ``Neural Surfer,'' a trading strategy that exploits the velocity of the latent state to detect market instability. Backtesting on the 2008 financial crisis and the 2023-2025 period shows that the Neural Operator successfully anticipates regime shifts, offering a physics-informed alternative to traditional risk management.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The Inverse Problem of Market State Estimation}
Quantitative finance is fundamentally an \textit{inverse problem} \citep{bouchouev1997}: given a set of observed prices \(\mathcal{Y}\), we seek to infer the unobservable state parameters \(\mathcal{Z}\) (e.g., volatility, correlation, drift) that generated them. Traditional parametric models, such as Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) or Heston, assume a fixed functional form \(\mathcal{F}\) (a Stochastic Partial Differential Equation, SPDE) and solve for the parameters \(\theta\) that minimize the pricing error: \(\theta^* = \text{argmin}_\theta \norm{\mathcal{F}(\theta) - \mathcal{Y}}\).

However, this approach suffers from a critical limitation: it assumes the \emph{physics} of the market \(\mathcal{F}\) is static and known. In reality, financial markets are \emph{Open Systems} where the governing laws themselves evolve—a phenomenon known as Regime Switching \citep{hamilton1989}. As argued by the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis \citep{lo2004adaptive}, market dynamics are evolutionary, driven by the interactions of adaptive agents. During a crisis, the market does not merely experience ``high volatility'' (a parameter change); the correlations and feedback loops (the equations themselves) fundamentally alter. We frame this as a \textbf{State Estimation Problem} on a latent manifold: can we learn a mapping that projects high-dimensional, noisy market observables onto a low-dimensional \emph{Regime Manifold} \(\mathcal{M}\), where the coordinates correspond to the true, evolving parameters of the underlying SPDE?

\subsection{Why Neural Operators?}
Standard deep learning approaches (MLPs, LSTMs) map finite-dimensional vectors to vectors. However, market states are better conceptualized as \textit{functions}—continuous trajectories of macro-financial variables over time. Similarly, the output (an option price surface) is a function of strike and maturity.
\textbf{Neural Operators} \citep{shen2025neuralsdesunifiedapproach} are designed specifically for this \textit{function-to-function} mapping. Unlike standard networks, they learn a discretization-invariant operator \(\mathcal{G}: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{U}\). This is theoretically crucial for two reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Resolution Invariance}: The model should yield consistent inferences whether we sample data daily, hourly, or by the minute.
    \item \textbf{Infinite-Dimensionality}: The space of possible market regimes is infinite-dimensional. Neural Operators approximate the operator on the function space, not just the specific training samples.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Heston SPDE as a Canonical Testbed}
To rigorously validate this framework, we cannot rely solely on empirical data, where the \emph{ground truth} regime is unknown. We instead employ the Heston Stochastic Volatility Model \citep{heston1993} as a \emph{Canonical Physics} testbed. The Heston SPDE provides a controlled environment where the regime is explicitly defined by the stochastic variance process \(v_t\). If our Neural Operator can recover the topology of the Heston parameter space purely from option prices—without being told the equations—it serves as a proof-of-principle that the method can extract \emph{financial physics} from data.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{The Market Manifold Hypothesis}
We posit that the observable market state \(x_t \in \bb{R}^N\) (prices, volumes) is a noisy projection of a latent state \(z_t\) evolving on a low-dimensional Riemannian manifold \(\mathcal{M}\).
\[
    x_t = \Phi(z_t) + \epsilon_t, \quad z_t \in \mathcal{M} \subset \bb{R}^d
\]
The Regime Classification problem is thus equivalent to learning the coordinate chart of \(\mathcal{M}\). Stable markets correspond to regions of \(\mathcal{M}\) with low curvature (flat geometry), while crises correspond to regions of high curvature or topological singularities.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation of the Inverse Problem}
Let \(\mathcal{F}: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{Y}\) be the forward pricing operator mapping latent regimes \(z \in \mathcal{Z}\) to option price surfaces \(y \in \mathcal{Y}\). The inverse problem is to find \(z\) such that \(\mathcal{F}(z) = y_{\text{market}}\). This problem is typically \textbf{ill-posed} in the sense of Hadamard.

To address this, we employ a \textbf{Tikhonov-style regularization} approach \citep{engl1996regularization}. We seek an approximation \(z_\theta = E_\theta(x)\) that minimizes:
\[
    J(\theta) = \norm{\mathcal{F}(E_\theta(x)) - y_{\text{market}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}^2 + \lambda \mathcal{R}(E_\theta(x))
\]
where \(\mathcal{R}\) imposes physical constraints (e.g., No-Arbitrage). Our Neural Operator effectively learns the regularized inverse map \(E_\theta \approx \mathcal{F}^\dagger\).

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data Generation: The Heston World}
To rigorously validate our Neural Operator framework, we require a dataset that combines the complexity of real-world market drivers with the mathematical tractability of a known ground truth. We construct a semi-synthetic \emph{Heston World} dataset by driving a stochastic volatility simulation with empirical market data.

\subsubsection{Empirical Data Sourcing}
We source daily historical market data from \textbf{Yahoo Finance} via the \texttt{yfinance} API, covering the period from January 1, 2006, to January 1, 2025. This 19-year window captures multiple distinct market regimes, including the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the 2010 Flash Crash, the prolonged Bull Market of the 2010s, the 2020 COVID-19 Crash, and the subsequent recovery and inflation period.

The dataset comprises two primary components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Equity Indices}: We aggregate price and volume data for four major US equity indices to ensure a representative sample of market behavior: the \textbf{S\&P 500} (\texttt{\^{}GSPC}), the \textbf{Nasdaq 100} (\texttt{\^{}NDX}), the \textbf{Russell 2000} (\texttt{\^{}RUT}), and the \textbf{Dow Jones Industrial Average} (\texttt{\^{}DJI}). For each index, we extract the daily `Close` price and `Volume`.
    \item \textbf{Macro-Financial Indicators}: To capture the broader economic environment, we incorporate key macro-financial variables:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{CBOE Volatility Index} (\texttt{\^{}VIX}): A proxy for market sentiment and implied volatility.
        \item \textbf{10-Year Treasury Yield} (\texttt{\^{}TNX}): A benchmark for the risk-free rate and economic outlook.
        \item \textbf{Wilshire 5000 Total Market Index} (\texttt{\^{}W5000}): A proxy for total US market capitalization.
        \item \textbf{US GDP}: Quarterly Gross Domestic Product data sourced from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database, resampled to daily frequency via forward-filling to compute the ``Buffett Indicator'' (Market Cap / GDP).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Physics-Informed Simulation}
We use this empirical data to drive a Heston Stochastic Volatility model. The Heston dynamics are governed by the coupled system of SDEs:

The Heston dynamics are governed by the coupled system of SDEs:
\[
    d S_t = \mu S_t dt + \sqrt{v_t} S_t d W_t^S
\]
\[
    d v_t = \kappa\parens{\theta - v_t} dt + \xi \sqrt{v_t} d W_t^v
\]
where \(\bb{E}\brac{d W_t^S d W_t^v} = \rho dt\). We simulate these paths using an Euler-Maruyama discretization. For each trading day \(t\) in the historical record (2010-2025), we use the realized volatility of the index to initialize \(v_t\) and generate a corresponding option price surface \(\mathbf{Y}_t\) consisting of 21 contracts (3 maturities \(\times\) 7 strikes). This ensures our target data contains realistic features like volatility skew and smile, while remaining mathematically consistent.

\subsection{Neural Operator Architecture}
We implement the Neural Operator as a deep autoencoder architecture designed to compress the 30-day market history into a latent regime vector. This architecture is composed of two primary mappings: the Encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) and the Decoder \(\mathcal{D}\).

\subsubsection{The Encoder: Inverse Mapping}
The Encoder \(\mathcal{E}: \bb{R}^{30 \times 6} \to \bb{R}^3\) approximates the inverse operator \(\Phi^{-1}\). It processes a sliding window of 6 market features: Log Returns, Realized Volatility, VIX, Log Volume, 10-Year Treasury Yield (TNX), and the Buffett Indicator. The input tensor of shape \((30, 6)\) is first flattened into a vector of dimension 180. This vector is then passed through a 4-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with the following specifications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Layer 1}: Linear(180 \(\to\) 128) + Batch Normalization + ReLU
    \item \textbf{Layer 2}: Linear(128 \(\to\) 64) + Batch Normalization + ReLU
    \item \textbf{Layer 3}: Linear(64 \(\to\) 32) + Batch Normalization + ReLU
    \item \textbf{Layer 4}: Linear(32 \(\to\) 3) (Latent Bottleneck)
\end{itemize}
The output of this network is the latent state vector \(z_t \in \bb{R}^3\), which represents the coordinates of the market regime on the manifold \(\mathcal{M}\).

\subsubsection{The Decoder: Forward Pricing Operator}
The Decoder \(\mathcal{D}: \bb{R}^3 \to \bb{R}^{21}\) approximates the forward pricing operator \(\mathcal{F}\). It maps the latent vector \(z_t\) back to the option price surface \(\mathbf{Y}_t\). The Decoder mirrors the Encoder's structure but in reverse:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Layer 1}: Linear(3 \(\to\) 32) + Batch Normalization + ReLU
    \item \textbf{Layer 2}: Linear(32 \(\to\) 64) + Batch Normalization + ReLU
    \item \textbf{Layer 3}: Linear(64 \(\to\) 128) + Batch Normalization + ReLU
    \item \textbf{Layer 4}: Linear(128 \(\to\) 21) + \textbf{Softplus}
\end{itemize}
Crucially, the final layer uses a \textbf{Softplus} activation function, defined as \(f(x) = \ln(1 + e^x)\). This choice is theoretically motivated by the financial requirement that option prices must be strictly positive, \(C(K) > 0\). Unlike ReLU, which can output exact zeros (leading to ``dead'' gradients), Softplus ensures a smooth, non-zero gradient flow even for deep out-of-the-money options.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Loss Function}
To ensure the learned manifold respects financial laws, we minimize a composite loss function that balances data fidelity with physical consistency:
\[
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSLE}} + \lambda_{\text{arb}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}}
\]

\subsubsection{Reconstruction Loss (MSLE)}
The primary objective is to minimize the reconstruction error between the predicted option prices \(\hat{C}\) and the target prices \(C\). We employ the Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (MSLE):
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSLE}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \parens{\ln(\hat{C}_i + 1) - \ln(C_i + 1)}^2
\]
We choose MSLE over the standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) due to the wide dynamic range of option prices. Deep in-the-money options may trade at \$100, while deep out-of-the-money options trade at \$0.05. An MSE loss would be dominated by the expensive options, causing the model to neglect the cheap ones. MSLE penalizes relative errors, ensuring that the model learns to price the entire surface with equal precision.

\subsubsection{Arbitrage Penalty (Regularization)}
The \textbf{Arbitrage Penalty} \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}}\) enforces the monotonicity of call prices with respect to strike price. A fundamental no-arbitrage condition for call options is that price must be a non-increasing function of strike: \(\partial C / \partial K \le 0\). For a set of sorted strikes \(K_1 < K_2 < \dots < K_N\), this implies \(C(K_i) \ge C(K_{i+1})\). We penalize violations using a ReLU-based energy function:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \text{ReLU}\parens{C(K_{i+1}) - C(K_i)}
\]
This penalty term guides the optimization toward the submanifold of valid pricing functions, acting as a soft constraint during training. In the context of inverse problem theory, \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}}\) serves as the regularization term \(\mathcal{R}\) in the Tikhonov functional, ensuring that the recovered solution is not just data-consistent but also physically admissible. \section{Results: Latent Space Analysis}

\subsection{Topological Clustering and Regime Separation}
To validate the ``Market Manifold Hypothesis,'' we analyze the geometric structure of the learned latent space \(\mathcal{Z} \subset \bb{R}^3\). We employ Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to project the 3-dimensional latent vectors onto a 2D plane for visualization. Figure \ref{fig:latent_space} reveals the intrinsic topology of the market regime manifold.

The manifold exhibits a striking ``comet-like'' structure that organizes market states based on their stability. The \textbf{Stable Core} (colored in blue) consists of low-volatility regimes (VIX \(< 15\)). These points cluster tightly in a dense, central region, representing the ``normal'' functioning of the market where dynamics are mean-reverting and predictable. This corresponds to the ``basin of attraction'' of the stable equilibrium.

In sharp contrast, the \textbf{Crisis Tails} (colored in red) consist of high-volatility regimes (VIX \(> 30\)). These points do not form a dense cluster but rather extend outward from the core in long, sparse trajectories. These trajectories represent the market's excursion away from equilibrium during shock events. The topological distinction is clear: ``Crisis'' is not merely a region of high variance, but a fundamentally different dynamical state characterized by high velocity and transience.

\subsection{Latent Dynamics and Phase Transitions}
By tracing the trajectory of the market state \(z_t\) through the manifold, we observe that financial crises manifest as high-velocity phase transitions (Figure \ref{fig:trajectories}). During the 2008 Financial Crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 crash, the system undergoes a distinct ``excursion.'' First, an \textbf{Ejection} phase occurs where the state \(z_t\) rapidly accelerates away from the stable core. This is followed by a \textbf{Transient Orbit} as the system traverses the sparse ``Crisis'' region of the manifold. Finally, a \textbf{Relaxation} phase brings the state slowly spiraling back into the stable basin of attraction as volatility subsides.

\section{Application: Neural Trading Strategies}

\subsection{The Neural Surfer: Surfing the Tangent Bundle}
We propose a trading strategy that exploits the differential geometry of the learned manifold. We define the \textbf{Latent Velocity} \(v_t\) as the norm of the tangent vector \(\dot{z}_t\) in the latent space. In a discrete setting, we approximate this using the backward difference quotient:
\[
    v_t \approx \norm{\frac{z_t - z_{t-1}}{\Delta t}}_2
\]
This quantity serves as a scalar order parameter for the stability of the system. When the market is in a stable basin of attraction (e.g., a low-volatility bull market), \(v_t\) is small, dominated by idiosyncratic noise (Brownian motion on the manifold). However, during a regime shift (a phase transition), the system traverses the manifold rapidly to find a new equilibrium, causing a spike in \(v_t\).

The \textbf{Neural Surfer} strategy acts as a ``regime-change detector'' by monitoring this velocity. We define a dynamic threshold \(\tau_t\) as the \(q\)-th percentile of the rolling velocity history over a window \(W\). The trading signal \(S_t \in \{0, 1\}\) is given by:
\[
    S_t = \mathbb{I}(v_t \le \tau_t)
\]
where \(\mathbb{I}\) is the indicator function. This strategy effectively ``surfs'' the manifold, maintaining exposure during stable geodesic flows and exiting to cash when the system undergoes a high-velocity topological reconfiguration.

We evaluate the strategy on the S\&P 500 (\^{}GSPC) over two distinct periods: the \textbf{2008 Financial Crisis} (2006-2009) and the \textbf{Post-COVID Recovery} (2023-2025). We assume zero transaction costs for simplicity, but the low turnover of the strategy (switching only on regime shifts) makes this a reasonable approximation. We compare against a \textbf{Buy \& Hold} benchmark and a \textbf{BSM Baseline} strategy that uses the raw VIX level (exiting when VIX \(> 80\)th percentile) as a proxy for the ``perfect'' parametric strategy.

\section{Conclusion}

We have demonstrated that Neural Operators can effectively learn a low-dimensional manifold that captures the complex regimes of financial markets. The learned latent space is not just a compression, but a meaningful topological map where distance corresponds to regime similarity and velocity corresponds to instability. This framework offers a promising direction for ``Physics-Informed Finance,'' bridging the gap between rigorous SPDE modelling and data-driven deep learning.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
While promising, this work relies on a semi-synthetic ``Heston World'' for training, which assumes that the underlying physics of the market are well-approximated by stochastic volatility dynamics. Future work will focus on three key areas: \textbf{Empirical Calibration}, training directly on historical option chains (e.g., SPX options) to capture non-Heston features like rough volatility; \textbf{Universal Operators}, extending the architecture to learn a single operator across multiple asset classes (Equities, FX, Rates); and \textbf{Control}, formulating the optimal hedging problem as a control task on the learned manifold.
