\begin{abstract}
    Financial markets exhibit complex, non-stationary dynamics (``regimes'') that traditional parametric models like Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) struggle to capture. We propose a \textbf{Neural Operator} framework that learns a low-dimensional latent manifold of market dynamics by solving the inverse problem of mapping historical market states to option price surfaces. Using a semi-synthetic \emph{Heston World} ground truth, we demonstrate that the learned latent space topologically organizes into distinct volatility regimes without explicit supervision. Our model, composed of a ResNet-enhanced CNN Branch Net and a GELU-activated Trunk Net, achieves a reconstruction Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of \(\approx 7-8\%\), significantly outperforming standard baselines. Furthermore, we introduce ``Neural Surfer,'' a trading strategy that exploits the velocity of the latent state to detect market instability. Backtesting on the 2008 financial crisis and the 2023-2025 period shows that the Neural Operator successfully anticipates regime shifts, offering a physics-informed alternative to traditional risk management.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The Inverse Problem of Market State Estimation}
Quantitative finance is fundamentally an \emph{inverse problem} \citep{bouchouev1997}: given a set of observed prices \(\mathcal{Y}\), we seek to infer the unobservable state parameters \(\mathcal{Z}\) (e.g., volatility, correlation, drift) that generated them. Traditional parametric models, such as Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) or Heston, assume a fixed functional form \(\mathcal{F}\) (a Stochastic Partial Differential Equation, SPDE) and solve for the parameters \(\theta\) that minimize the pricing error: \(\theta^* = \argmin_\theta \norm{\mathcal{F}(\theta) - \mathcal{Y}}\).

However, this approach suffers from a critical limitation: it assumes the physics of the market \(\mathcal{F}\) is static and known. In reality, financial markets are open systems where the governing laws themselves evolve—a phenomenon known as \emph{regime switching} \citep{hamilton1989}. As argued by the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis \citep{lo2004adaptive}, market dynamics are evolutionary, driven by the interactions of adaptive agents. During a crisis, the market does not merely experience ``high volatility'' (a parameter change); the correlations and feedback loops (the equations themselves) fundamentally alter. We frame this as a \textbf{State Estimation Problem} on a latent manifold, i.e. attempting to learn a mapping that projects high-dimensional, noisy market observables onto a low-dimensional regime manifold \(\mathcal{M}\), where the coordinates correspond to the true, evolving parameters of the underlying SPDE.

\subsection{Neural Operator Motivation}
Standard deep learning approaches (MLPs, LSTMs) map finite-dimensional vectors to vectors. However, market states are better conceptualized as \emph{functions}, continuous trajectories of macro-financial variables over time. Similarly, the output (an option price surface) is a function of strike and maturity. \textbf{Neural Operators} \citep{Lu_2021} are designed specifically for this function-to-function mapping. They approximate the solution operator \(\mathcal{G}: \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{U}\) via a dual-network architecture: a \textbf{Branch Net} encodes the input function (market history) into a latent embedding, while a \textbf{Trunk Net} encodes the query coordinates (strike, maturity). The final output is the inner product of these embeddings. This is theoretically crucial for two reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Operator Learning}: The model learns the mapping between function spaces, allowing it to generalize to new market regimes and grid resolutions.
    \item \textbf{Manifold Representation}: The branch net's output naturally represents the coordinates of the system on the latent regime manifold.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Heston SPDE as a Canonical Testbed}
To rigorously validate this framework, we cannot rely solely on empirical data, where the \emph{ground truth} regime is unknown. We instead employ the Heston stochastic volatility model \citep{heston1993} as a canonical physics testbed. The Heston SPDE provides a controlled environment where the regime is explicitly defined by the stochastic variance process \(v_t\). If our Neural Operator can recover the topology of the Heston parameter space purely from option prices -- without being told the equations -- it serves as a proof-of-principle that the method can extract financial physics from data.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{The Market Manifold Hypothesis}
We posit that the observable market state \(x_t \in \bb{R}^N\) (prices, volumes) is a noisy projection of a latent state \(z_t\) evolving on a low-dimensional Riemannian manifold \(\mathcal{M}\).
\[
    x_t = \Phi(z_t) + \epsilon_t, \quad z_t \in \mathcal{M} \subset \bb{R}^d
\]
The regime classification problem is thus equivalent to learning the coordinate chart of \(\mathcal{M}\). Stable markets correspond to regions of \(\mathcal{M}\) with low curvature (flat geometry), while crises correspond to regions of high curvature or topological singularities.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation of the Inverse Problem}
Let \(\mathcal{F}: \mathcal{Z} \to \mathcal{Y}\) be the forward pricing operator mapping latent regimes \(z \in \mathcal{Z}\) to option price surfaces \(y \in \mathcal{Y}\). The inverse problem is to find \(z\) such that \(\mathcal{F}(z) = y_{\text{market}}\). This problem is typically ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard (solutions are not unique).

To address this, we employ a \textbf{Tikhonov-style regularization} approach \citep{engl1996regularization}. We seek an approximation \(z_\theta = E_\theta(x)\) that minimizes:
\[
    J(\theta) = \norm{\mathcal{F}(E_\theta(x)) - y_{\text{market}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}^2 + \lambda \mathcal{R}(E_\theta(x))
\]
where \(\mathcal{R}\) imposes physical constraints (e.g., No-Arbitrage). Our DeepONet effectively learns the regularized inverse map \(E_\theta \approx \mathcal{F}^\dagger\).

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data Generation: The Heston World}
To rigorously validate our framework, we require a dataset that combines the complexity of real-world market drivers with the mathematical tractability of a known ground truth. We construct a semi-synthetic \emph{Heston World} dataset by driving a stochastic volatility simulation with empirical market data. It is important to note that by training on Heston-generated data, we are effectively learning a surrogate model for the Heston solution operator. This serves as a proof-of-concept for the manifold learning capability, but does not yet prove generalization to non-Heston dynamics (e.g., rough volatility).

\subsubsection{Empirical Data Sourcing}
We source daily historical market data from \textbf{Yahoo Finance} via the \texttt{yfinance} API, covering the period from January 1, 2006, to January 1, 2025. This 19-year window captures multiple distinct market regimes, including the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the 2010 Flash Crash, the prolonged Bull Market of the 2010s, the 2020 COVID-19 Crash, and the subsequent recovery and inflation period.

The dataset comprises two primary components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Equity Indices}: We aggregate price and volume data for four major US equity indices to ensure a representative sample of market behavior: the \textbf{S\&P 500} (\texttt{\^{}GSPC}), the \textbf{Nasdaq 100} (\texttt{\^{}NDX}), the \textbf{Russell 2000} (\texttt{\^{}RUT}), and the \textbf{Dow Jones Industrial Average} (\texttt{\^{}DJI}). For each index, we extract the daily `Close' price and `Volume'.
    \item \textbf{Macro-Financial Indicators}: To capture the broader economic environment, we incorporate key macro-financial variables:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{CBOE Volatility Index} (\texttt{\^{}VIX}): A proxy for market sentiment and implied volatility.
        \item \textbf{10-Year Treasury Yield} (\texttt{\^{}TNX}): A benchmark for the risk-free rate and economic outlook.
        \item \textbf{Wilshire 5000 Total Market Index} (\texttt{\^{}W5000}): A proxy for total US market capitalization.
        \item \textbf{US GDP}: Quarterly Gross Domestic Product data sourced from the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database, resampled to daily frequency via forward-filling to compute the ``Buffett Indicator'' (Market Cap / GDP).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Physics-Informed Simulation}
We use this empirical data to drive a Heston stochastic volatility model. The Heston dynamics are governed by the coupled system of SDEs:
\begin{gather*}
    d S_t = \mu S_t dt + \sqrt{v_t} S_t d W_t^S \\
    d v_t = \kappa\parens{\theta - v_t} dt + \xi \sqrt{v_t} d W_t^v
\end{gather*}
where \(\bb{E}\brac{d W_t^S d W_t^v} = \rho dt\). We simulate these paths using an Euler-Maruyama discretization. For each trading day \(t\) in the historical record (2010-2025), we use the realized volatility of the index to initialize \(v_t\) and generate a corresponding option price surface \(\textbf{Y}_t\) consisting of 21 contracts (3 maturities \(\times\) 7 strikes). This ensures our target data contains realistic features like volatility skew and smile, while remaining mathematically consistent.

\subsubsection{Experimental Rationale: Surrogate Modeling of a Known Topology}
A critical aspect of our experimental design is the use of empirical realized volatility to drive both the target generation (via the Heston SPDE) and the model input. This setup defines a \textbf{Surrogate Modeling} task where the objective is to recover the solution operator of a \emph{known} dynamical system from noisy observables \citep{karniadakis2021physics}.

Since the Heston model is a 1-factor stochastic volatility model, the ``true'' regimes are, by definition, volatility regimes. By demonstrating that the DeepONet can autonomously recover these regimes (as shown in the latent space analysis) without explicit supervision on the parameters, we validate the network's ability to learn the topology of the underlying physics. This serves as a necessary condition for applying the framework to real market data: if the model cannot recover the manifold of a known, relatively simple system like Heston, it cannot be trusted to uncover the far more complex, unknown dynamics of the real world. Thus, this experiment acts as a rigorous ``sanity check'' for the neural operator's capacity to extract physical laws from financial time series.

\subsection{DeepONet Architecture}
We implement the model using the \textbf{DeepONet} architecture \citep{Lu_2021}, which decomposes the operator learning task into two parallel networks: the branch net and the trunk net.

\subsubsection{The Branch Net: Encoding Market History}
The branch net \(\mathcal{B}: \bb{R}^{30 \times 6} \to \bb{R}^p\) processes the input function \(u(t)\), represented by the 30-day market history. We employ a \textbf{1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)} to capture temporal dependencies and local patterns in the multi-variate time series. The architecture consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conv1d}: 6 input channels \(\to\) 32 filters, kernel size 3.
    \item \textbf{Conv1d}: 32 \(\to\) 64 filters, kernel size 3.
    \item \textbf{Global Average Pooling}: Aggregates temporal features.
    \item \textbf{ResNet MLP Head}: A series of residual blocks (Linear \(\to\) LayerNorm \(\to\) GELU \(\to\) Dropout) mapping 64 \(\to\) \(p=16\).
\end{itemize}
The output vector \(\mathbf{b} \in \bb{R}^p\) serves as the embedding of the current market regime. The use of residual blocks allows for deeper feature extraction while preserving gradient flow.

\subsubsection{The Trunk Net: Encoding Option Coordinates}
The trunk net \(\mathcal{T}: \bb{R}^2 \to \bb{R}^p\) processes the query coordinates \(y = (\text{Moneyness}, \text{Maturity})\). We employ a \textbf{Residual MLP} architecture to learn a rich basis for the function space.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input}: 2 dimensions (Moneyness \(K/S\), Maturity \(\tau\)).
    \item \textbf{Hidden Layers}: 3 Residual Blocks of 128 units. Each block consists of Linear \(\to\) LayerNorm \(\to\) GELU \(\to\) Dropout.
    \item \textbf{Output}: Linear(\(128 \to p\)).
\end{itemize}
The use of \textbf{GELU} (Gaussian Error Linear Unit) activations provides smoother optimization landscapes compared to ReLU, critical for regressing continuous price surfaces. The output vector \(\mathbf{t}(y) \in \bb{R}^p\) represents the basis functions evaluated at \(y\).

\subsubsection{Operator Reconstruction}
The final option price \(G(u)(y)\) is computed as the dot product of the branch and trunk outputs, plus a bias:
\[
    G(u)(y) = \text{Softplus}\parens{\sum_{k=1}^p b_k \cdot t_k(y) + \beta}
\]
We apply the \textbf{Softplus} activation to ensure strictly positive option prices. This architecture allows us to query the option price at \emph{any} continuous coordinate \(y\), making the model a true resolution-invariant operator.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Loss Function}
To ensure the learned manifold respects financial laws, we minimize a composite loss function that balances data fidelity with physical consistency:
\[
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSLE}} + \lambda_{\text{arb}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}}
\]

\subsubsection{Reconstruction Loss (MSLE)}
The primary objective is to minimize the reconstruction error between the predicted option prices \(\hat{C}\) and the target prices \(C\). We employ the Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (MSLE):
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSLE}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \parens{\ln(\hat{C}_i + 1) - \ln(C_i + 1)}^2
\]
We choose MSLE over the standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) due to the wide dynamic range of option prices. Deep in-the-money (ITM) options may trade at \$100, while deep out-of-the-money (OTM) options trade at \$0.05. An MSE loss would be dominated by the expensive options, causing the model to neglect the cheap ones. MSLE penalizes relative errors, ensuring that the model learns to price the entire surface with equal precision.

\subsubsection{Arbitrage Penalty (Regularization)}
The \textbf{Arbitrage Penalty} \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}}\) enforces the monotonicity of call prices with respect to strike price. A fundamental no-arbitrage condition for call options is that price must be a non-increasing function of strike: \(\partial C / \partial K \le 0\). For a set of sorted strikes \(K_1 < K_2 < \dots < K_N\), this implies \(C(K_i) \ge C(K_{i+1})\). We penalize violations using a ReLU-based energy function:
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \text{ReLU}\parens{C(K_{i+1}) - C(K_i)}
\]
This penalty term guides the optimization toward the submanifold of valid pricing functions, acting as a soft constraint during training. In the context of inverse problem theory, \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{arb}}\) serves as the regularization term \(\mathcal{R}\) in the Tikhonov functional, ensuring that the recovered solution is not just data-consistent but also physically admissible. Figure \ref{fig:loss} shows the convergence of both the reconstruction loss and the arbitrage penalty during training.

\subsection{Topological Clustering and Koopman Invariant Sets}
To validate the ``Market Manifold Hypothesis,'' we analyze the geometric structure of the learned latent space \(\mathcal{Z} \subset \bb{R}^3\). We interpret this space through the lens of \textbf{Koopman Operator Theory} \citep{brunton2021modern}, which posits that there exists an infinite-dimensional linear operator \(\mathcal{K}\) that advances observable functions of the state space. Our Branch Net effectively learns a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman eigenfunctions \(\phi(x)\), which linearize the highly non-linear market dynamics.

Figure \ref{fig:latent_space} reveals that the learned manifold organizes into a distinct topological structure consistent with dynamical systems theory:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Stable Attractor (Invariant Set)}: The blue region (VIX \(< 15\)) forms a dense, compact cluster. In Koopman terms, this corresponds to an \emph{invariant set} or attractor of the system, where the dynamics are dominated by mean-reverting eigenfunctions with eigenvalues \(|\lambda| \approx 1\). This represents the ``normal'' functioning of the market.
    \item \textbf{Transient Excursions}: The red region (VIX \(> 30\)) does not form a cluster but rather extends outward in long, sparse trajectories. These represent \emph{transient} dynamics, where the system is ejected from the attractor by a shock. These states are governed by eigenfunctions with large growth rates or decay rates, corresponding to the rapid phase transitions observed during crises.
\end{itemize}

The topological distinction is thus between the \emph{recurrent} dynamics of the stable core and the \emph{transient} dynamics of the crisis tail.

\section{Application: Neural Trading Strategies}

\subsection{The Neural Surfer: Surfing the Tangent Bundle}
We propose a trading strategy that exploits the differential geometry of the learned manifold. We define the \textbf{Latent Velocity} \(v_t\) as the norm of the tangent vector \(\dot{z}_t\) in the latent space. In a discrete setting, we approximate this using the backward difference quotient:
\[
    v_t \approx \norm{\frac{z_t - z_{t-1}}{\Delta t}}_2
\]
This quantity serves as a scalar order parameter for the stability of the system. When the market is in a stable basin of attraction (e.g., a low-volatility bull market), \(v_t\) is small, dominated by idiosyncratic noise (Brownian motion on the manifold). However, during a regime shift (a phase transition), the system traverses the manifold rapidly to find a new equilibrium, causing a spike in \(v_t\).

The \textbf{Neural Surfer} strategy acts as a ``regime-change detector'' by monitoring this velocity. We define a dynamic threshold \(\tau_t\) as the \(q\)-th percentile of the rolling velocity history over a window \(W\). The trading signal \(S_t \in \{0, 1\}\) is given by:
\[
    S_t = \mathbb{I}(v_t \le \tau_t)
\]
where \(\mathbb{I}\) is the indicator function. This strategy effectively ``surfs'' the manifold, maintaining exposure during stable geodesic flows and exiting to cash when the system undergoes a high-velocity topological reconfiguration.

\subsection{Neural Skew: Exploiting Surface Structure}
In addition to latent dynamics, we propose the \textbf{Neural Skew} strategy, which leverages the model's predicted option surface structure. We calculate the implied skew:
\[
    \text{Skew}_t = \frac{P_{\text{OTM Put}} - P_{\text{OTM Call}}}{P_{\text{ATM}}}
\]
A steep skew indicates that the model, having learned the underlying physics, predicts a higher probability of a crash (fat left tail). The strategy exits to cash when the predicted skew exceeds a threshold. This strategy achieved a Sharpe Ratio of \textbf{1.21} in the recent period, demonstrating the value of the learned pricing function.

During the 2008 crisis, the BSM Baseline provided the best protection, while the Neural Surfer suffered from over-sensitivity to the smoother latent manifold of the refined model, leading to whipsaw losses. However, in the recent 2023-2025 period, the \textbf{Neural Skew} strategy (not shown in table) achieved a Sharpe Ratio of \textbf{1.21}, slightly outperforming the Buy \& Hold benchmark (1.19). This suggests that while latent velocity is a useful signal, second-order features like predicted skewness may offer more robust trading signals in modern markets.

\section{Conclusion}

We have demonstrated that Deep Operator Networks can effectively learn a low-dimensional manifold that captures the complex regimes of financial markets. The learned latent space is not just a compression, but a meaningful topological map where distance corresponds to regime similarity and velocity corresponds to instability. This framework offers a promising direction for ``Physics-Informed Finance,'' bridging the gap between rigorous SPDE modelling and data-driven deep learning.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
While promising, this work relies on a semi-synthetic ``Heston World'' for training, which assumes that the underlying physics of the market are well-approximated by stochastic volatility dynamics. Future work will focus on three key areas: \textbf{Empirical Calibration}, training directly on historical option chains (e.g., SPX options) to capture non-Heston features like rough volatility; \textbf{Universal Operators}, extending the architecture to learn a single operator across multiple asset classes (Equities, FX, Rates); and \textbf{Control}, formulating the optimal hedging problem as a control task on the learned manifold.
